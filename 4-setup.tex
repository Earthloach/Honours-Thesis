\chapter{A Calculus for Worst-Case Cost Analysis}

This Chapter presents $\lambda^g_{\mathsf{worst}}$, a calculus for worst-case cost analysis of guarded recursive programs.
The language's type system consists of the later modality for guarded recursion and a cost monad for cost analysis.
Specifically, the language adapts fragments from the guarded lambda calculus of \cite{cloustonGuardedLambdaCalculusProgramming2017a}
and $\lambda$-amor of \cite{rajaniUnifyingTypetheoryHigherorder2021a}.

Firstly, in Section \ref{sec:cost-foundation} we look at the interaction between gradeds and the later modality, 
which results in the notion of \emph{time-dependent cost}, as a foundation of cost analysis in $\lambda^g_{\mathsf{worst}}$. 
We then describe and detail the type system, syntax and semantics of the calculus in Section \ref{sec:worst-case-formalisation}.
Finally, we demonstrate the expresiveness of the language and its type system through program examples.

\section{Quantative foundations of $\lambda^g_{\mathsf{worst}}$}
\label{sec:cost-foundation}
Following $\lambda$-amor, we use a graded monad to represent cost-aware computations. For example, the type $\tmonad{3}{\tau}$ 
describes a computation whose result has type $\tau$, and the upper bound of the resulting cost is 3. 
More generally, the grade annotation of the monad is a \emph{nonnegative} real number.

Guarded recursion employs a notion of \emph{time}, represented by the later modality $\triangleright$ \citep{nakanoModalityRecursion2000}. 
For example, data of type $\tau$ is considered available now, while data of type $\triangleright \tau$ becomes accessible after one time step. 
In other words, an implcit time clock presents at type level, where entering the $\triangleright$ means that the clock ticks once.
Therefore, combining with the graded monad, we can leverage this "time-aware" feature of the later modality to express costs that vary along the \emph{time}.

Note that some work presents the clock explicitly \citep{atkeyProductiveCoprogrammingGuarded2013a,mogelberg14, bahrClocksAreTicking2017}, and
multiple clocks can exist, i.e. types like $\triangleright^\alpha$ and $\triangleright^\beta$ where $\alpha, \beta$ denotes 2 different clocks,
thus the notion of time varies between different clocks. 
Follwing the guarded lambda calculus, we only consider a single global clock in our type system. This also ensures that all costs 
change uniformly as time passes.


\subsection{Time-dependent cost}
Firstly, we use a abstract parameter $i\in\mathbb N$ to denote the current time, starting from $0$. Advancing one time step corresponds to moving from $i$ to $i+1$. 
We aim to spcify non-uniform costs that depends on $i$. For instance, a cost of $i+2$ 
gives costs 2, 3, 4 \dots as $i$ advances to $i+1$, $i+2$ \dots, while a constant cost like 3 remains unchanged as $i$ increments.

Similar to $\lambda$-amor, we use an index expression language to express quantatitive information for different types, such as the graded monad.

\begin{defn}(Index expressions)
\label{def:index}
$
  \\
  \begin{aligned}
    I,J ::= {}
    \kappa \mid N \mid R
    \mid I+J \mid I-J
    \mid \textstyle\sum_{\kappa<J} I
    \mid \mathsf{clk}
    \mid \mathsf S^\mathsf{n}(I)
  \end{aligned}
$
\end{defn}

Indices are written as $I$ and $J$, and all indices can depend on the time $i$, below we define the time interpretation of indices.

\begin{defn}(Time interpretations of indices)
\label{def:time-indices}
  \begin{gather*}
    \begin{alignedat}{2}
      \clk(i) &= i
        &\qquad N(i) &= N \\
      R(i) &= R
        &\qquad \kappa(i) &= \kappa \\[0.6em]
      (I-J)(i) &= I(i) - J(i)
        &\qquad (\mathsf{S}^n I)(i) &= I(i+n)
    \end{alignedat}
    \\[0.8em]
    \begin{aligned}
       (\textstyle\sum_{\kappa<J}I)(i)
        &=\sum_{k=0}^{J(i)-1}I[k/\kappa](i).
    \end{aligned}
  \end{gather*}
\end{defn}
We explain the definitions and interpretations of index expressions. From Definition \ref{def:time-indices}, all index expressions can be considered as schedules, or functions from the time 
$i$ to a \emph{nonnegative} real number. The index $\clk$ means the clock, i.e. the value of the current time $i$. As the time clock ticks, $i$ 
increments and the interpretation $\clk$ changes.

The indices $N, R$ and $\kappa$ represents constant functions on $i$. $N$ is a natural-number constant, $R$ is a \emph{nonnegative} real-number constant, 
and $\kappa$ denotes an index variable. Consequently, their interpretation are all constants, i.e. their original value.
The interpretations of addition and subtraction between indices are defined compositionally.
The $\mathsf{S^n}I$ expression shifts the time forward by $n$, then the interpretation of the inner $I$ becomes $I(i+n)$, this requires a side condition enforced in Section \ref{sec:worst-case-formalisation}, that
$n$ is a time-independent constant since it is also a index. $\mathsf{S^n}I$ allows us to express costs in the future, its usage will be demonstrated in Section \ref{sec:worst-case-formalisation}.

Finally, the index expression $\textstyle\sum_{\kappa<J} I$ describes bounded summation over indices, which sums $I$ 
from 0 to the interpretation of $(J - 1)$ inclusive, where $I$ may have free index variables inside, i.e. $I[0/\kappa] + \ldots + I[(J(i)-1)\kappa]$.

\subsection{Examples}
Now we can combine the graded monad with the later modality in the type system.
For example, a computation of type $\tmonad{\clk}{\tau}$ produces a \emph{symbolic} cost $\clk$, and the real cost will be the value of 
$i$. Usually we consider the time starts from 0, thus the real cost is 0. By contrast, a computation of $\triangleright\tmonad{\clk}{\tau}$ means that it
will be executed one step later due to the later modality, and the cost will be the value of $i+1 = 1$ since time has passed when the cost is produced.
Likewise, the cost will be $n$ for a computation of type $\triangleright^n \tmonad{\kappa}{\tau}$, where $n$ is the number of nested $\triangleright$.

On the other hand, computations of types $\tmonad{3}{\tau}$ and $\triangleright^n \tmonad{3}{\tau}$ do not distinguish their costs, while only the availabilities of their result data
differ.
